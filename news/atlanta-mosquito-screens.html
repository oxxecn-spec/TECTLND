Atlanta – America's Former #1 Mosquito City

Atlanta's mosquito legacy is unmatched. For six consecutive years, from 2015 through 2020, Atlanta held the top spot on Orkin's annual mosquito cities list.[citation:1] While Los Angeles has since taken that title, Atlanta remains a mosquito powerhouse at #5 nationwide in 2026.[citation:1][citation:6]

The ranking is based on the number of new residential mosquito treatments performed by pest control professionals over a one-year period.[citation:1] Atlanta's continued high ranking reflects the persistent mosquito pressure that defines life in the Southeast.

The Asian Tiger Mosquito – Atlanta's Invasive Legacy

Atlanta is the ground zero for the Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus) in the United States. The species was first discovered in the U.S. in the early 1980s, likely arriving in a shipment of used tires from Asia. In 1986, the CDC conducted a survey that found Aedes albopictus only in two locations: Atlanta (Fulton County) and Savannah, Georgia.[citation:9]

By 1991, the species had spread to all 159 counties in Georgia. By 1994, all 159 counties were confirmed infested.[citation:9] The commercial movement of used tires is implicated in this rapid dispersal, and adult mosquitoes have even been observed following humans and entering automobiles — demonstrating how human activity accelerates their spread.[citation:9]

Today, Aedes albopictus is established throughout the Southeast, and Atlanta remains a core habitat for this aggressive, daytime-biting mosquito. UGA research shows that urbanization, which alters microclimate and is often characterized by increasing surface imperviousness and heat island effects, increases Ae. albopictus abundance. Minimum relative humidity has a significant negative effect on adult abundance, while impervious surface coverage and daily temperature range have a significant positive effect.[citation:4]

Canopy cover also predicts greater larval habitat density, meaning Atlanta's tree-filled neighborhoods — while beautiful — create ideal conditions for Asian tiger mosquitoes to breed.[citation:4] The genetic structure of Ae. albopictus across Atlanta shows a single introduction event, likely from the initial invasive population in the early 1990s, followed by rapid population movement across the city — often leap-frogging over nearby sites.[citation:4]

🦟 The Asian Tiger Mosquito: Aedes albopictus is an aggressive daytime biter. Unlike most mosquitoes that feed at dusk and dawn, Asian tiger mosquitoes bite all day long. They are vectors for dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and West Nile virus. They breed in small containers — bottle caps, tires, flowerpots — making urban environments ideal habitat.[citation:9]

West Nile Virus – An Active Threat in Atlanta

West Nile virus is the most common mosquito-borne disease in the continental U.S., and Atlanta is at the center of the threat. In Georgia, the most important vector species are Culex quinquefasciatus, Aedes albopictus, and Culiseta melanura.[citation:3]

2025 West Nile Season in Atlanta

  • July 2025: Two mosquito traps in metro Atlanta tested positive for West Nile virus — in Hapeville and northwest Atlanta. Fulton County Board of Health confirmed the presence of infected mosquitoes in the area.[citation:11]
  • Late July 2025: Additional positive samples were recovered from traps in three locations: one each in the northwest, northeast, and southwest quadrants of the City of Atlanta.[citation:2][citation:7][citation:15]
  • August 2025: DeKalb Public Health confirmed two mosquito surveillance traps tested positive for West Nile virus in zip codes 30033 (Unincorporated DeKalb/NE Atlanta) and 30360 (Doraville).[citation:12]

Fulton County Board of Health officials urged residents to remember the "5 Ds" of mosquito prevention:

  • Dusk/Dawn — Avoid outdoor activities when mosquitoes are most active
  • Dress — Wear long sleeves and pants
  • DEET — Use insect repellent
  • Drain — Eliminate standing water
  • Doors — Ensure window and door screens are intact to prevent mosquitoes from entering the home[citation:7][citation:11]

A 2026 study at Berry College in northwest Georgia found that Aedes albopictus and Culex pipiens complex — both competent vectors of West Nile virus and Eastern Equine Encephalitis — were present at most sampling sites, with the majority at the Rollins Dairy.[citation:8] This confirms that mosquito-borne disease risk extends beyond Atlanta into surrounding regions.

Atlanta's Climate – A Mosquito Paradise

Atlanta's humid subtropical climate creates ideal conditions for mosquitoes:

  • Long mosquito season: Atlanta's warm weather extends from April through October, giving mosquitoes ample time to breed and multiply.
  • High humidity: The Southeast's humidity supports mosquito survival. UGA research found that lower minimum relative humidity reduces adult Ae. albopictus abundance — meaning Atlanta's high humidity increases mosquito populations.[citation:4]
  • Abundant rainfall: Atlanta receives about 50 inches of rain annually, creating countless standing water sources for mosquito breeding.
  • Tree canopy: Atlanta's "City in a Forest" nickname reflects its extensive tree cover — which also provides ideal habitat for mosquitoes. Canopy cover predicts greater larval habitat density.[citation:4]

These conditions mean that mosquitoes are a year-round concern in Atlanta. The city's warm, humid environment supports multiple mosquito species, including the Culex mosquitoes that spread West Nile virus and the Aedes mosquitoes that spread dengue and Zika.

Georgia Building Code – Screen Requirements

Georgia has adopted the International Property Maintenance Code (IPMC), which includes specific requirements for window screens. Section 304.14 of the IPMC states:

"Every door, window and other outside opening required for ventilation of habitable rooms... shall be supplied with approved tightly fitting screens of not less than 16 mesh per square inch and every swinging door shall have a self-closing device in good working condition."[citation:5]

The H01 meets and exceeds this requirement. With a mesh density of approximately 1.2mm openings (approx. 20 mesh per inch), it provides superior protection while maintaining airflow and visibility.
