Chicago – America's #2 Mosquito City

For several years, Chicago has ranked near the top of Orkin's annual mosquito list. In 2026, the city held its #2 position, behind only Los Angeles, and ahead of New York (#3), Detroit (#4), and Atlanta (#5)[citation:1][citation:5]. The ranking is based on the number of new residential mosquito treatments performed by pest control professionals over a one-year period from March 2025 to March 2026[citation:3].

The Midwest is seeing some of the fastest growth in mosquito activity year-over-year, with Milwaukee, Minneapolis, and downstate Springfield joining the list for the first time[citation:3]. Orkin entomologist Shannon Sked explained: "While major cities continue to experience consistent pressure, emerging trends — especially in the Midwest — show how shifting climate conditions and the expanding range of the yellow fever mosquito are creating new hot spots across the country"[citation:3].

Climate Change and Chicago's Lengthening Mosquito Season

Historically, Chicago's long, brutal winters provided a "hard reset on the mosquito population," according to Shannon LaDeau, a senior scientist at the Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies. But climate change is changing that[citation:11][citation:15].

As temperatures warm globally and Chicago's winters become milder, mosquito season is lengthening. Warmer temperatures also allow mosquitoes to reach adulthood faster, meaning they have more time to reproduce and their populations increase more quickly[citation:11][citation:15]. Chicago had 26 more "mosquito days" in 2022 than in 1979, according to a Climate Central analysis[citation:11][citation:15].

Shannon LaDeau explains the mechanism: "Because mosquitoes are ectotherms, everything about their physiology is temperature-dependent. Warmer temperatures speed up their growth and their replication of viruses"[citation:11][citation:15]. This means not just more mosquitoes, but also greater disease risk.

Urban mosquitoes are largely invasive species that have adapted to city life. As LaDeau notes: "Mosquitoes that emerge from standing water in a tire someone left on the side of the road have gotten adept at surviving urban areas and it gives them very close access to humans"[citation:11].

🌡️ Mosquito Disease Days Increase: Climate Central found that 95% of major US cities saw an increase in mosquito disease days — days warm enough for West Nile virus transmission. Chicago now experiences 26 more days suitable for mosquitoes than in 1979[citation:11].

West Nile Virus – Chicago's Growing Threat

West Nile virus is the most common cause of mosquito-borne disease in Chicago and the United States[citation:2]. In 2025, Chicago reported its first three human cases in August, with patients ranging in age from 40 to 80 years old residing on the Northwest and South sides[citation:2][citation:14]. The Illinois Department of Public Health had previously confirmed the first human case in the state on June 24.

The risk of West Nile virus in Chicago was rated as "high" in August 2025, with health officials noting "a higher proportion of mosquitoes testing positive for West Nile virus than usual"[citation:2][citation:14]. The Chicago Department of Public Health reported that several batches of mosquitoes tested positive for West Nile virus in June in at least 16 counties across the state, including Cook County[citation:14].

The numbers are significant. In 2024, suburban Cook County had 19 West Nile virus cases and five deaths from the virus — marking the highest number of county deaths due to the disease since 2018[citation:14]. Illinois as a state had nearly 70 human cases of West Nile virus in 2024, down from 119 in 2023 but still a serious public health concern[citation:14].

Dr. Olusimbo "Simbo" Ige, CDPH Commissioner, emphasized: "The best way to protect yourself from West Nile virus is to avoid mosquito bites and take measures to stop them from breeding around your home"[citation:2].

Why Urban Chicago Mosquitoes Are Hardier

One factor contributing to higher West Nile virus rates in Chicago may be the urban habitat. Justin Harbison, a Loyola University associate professor specializing in mosquito control research, explained: "Because so much of our mosquitoes that are transmitting West Nile virus grow up in these storm sewers that are really polluted … they become a lot more hardy and just stronger in general"[citation:3].

Chicago's storm drains provide an ideal breeding ground for the Culex pipiens mosquito — the primary vector for West Nile virus in urban areas. The city manages more than 80,000 catch basins with larvicide treatments to control mosquito populations[citation:2][citation:14].

Invasive species are also a growing concern. Scientists have seen a wider range of invasive species that carry viruses like dengue and yellow fever in recent years in Cook County[citation:3].

Chicago Building Code – Screen Requirements

Chicago homeowners need to know: the Chicago Building Code requires every door opening and every window to have screens in place from April 15 to November 15 of each year[citation:4][citation:8].

Specifically, the code requires:

  • Every door opening directly from any family unit to the outdoors must be supplied with a screen of not less than 16 mesh per inch[citation:4][citation:8]
  • Every window or other outside opening used for ventilation must be supplied with a screen
  • Every screen door shall have a self-closing device in good working condition

This code serves a dual purpose: keeping mosquitoes and other pests out, and preventing children from falling from windows. In 2014 alone, the Department of Buildings issued 1,526 violations for window-related issues[citation:8].

The H01 meets and exceeds the Chicago Building Code requirement. With a mesh density of approximately 1.2mm openings (approx. 20 mesh per inch), it provides superior protection while maintaining airflow and visibility.
