Understanding the Planning Framework

Before installing any screen, homeowners must understand the legal framework governing their property. The rules differ significantly between listed buildings and conservation areas.

Listed Buildings

Buildings in England and Wales are listed as Grade I, Grade II*, or Grade II. Scotland uses Category A, B, or C. Listed building consent (LBC) is required for any alteration that affects the building's special interest [citation:1][citation:8].

This includes both external and internal work. As one legal analysis noted, "even mundane internal works, such as replacing a solid door with a glass door... might require consent" [citation:8]. In a recent appeal, listed building consent was refused for TV screens installed in a medical practice because the screens, when seen from the street, had an "adverse effect on the character of each window and, in turn, the principal elevation of the building" [citation:8].

For listed buildings, external window screens or guards almost always require listed building consent [citation:1]. In one case, a Woodbridge woman was ordered to remove protective window bars from her Grade II-listed property because they were installed without listed building consent and were "deemed to be of poor design that harms the special interest of the listed building, in the conservation area" [citation:1].

Similarly, North Yorkshire Council's planning guidance states that external grilles "will not normally be acceptable unless it can be shown that security glass or internal grilles are inappropriate" [citation:6].

Conservation Areas

In conservation areas, planning controls are stricter than in standard areas but less stringent than for listed buildings. In Scotland, permitted development rights for altering or replacing windows in conservation areas changed in May 2024 [citation:2]:

  • If a window is on the principal elevation or a side elevation that fronts a road, and the design changes, prior approval is required [citation:2].
  • If the window is to the rear or a side elevation that does not front a road, no prior notification is required [citation:2].
  • For listed buildings, LBC is always required [citation:2].

In England, the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) Order 2015 (as amended) provides similar restrictions. Internal work — including internal screens — generally does not require planning permission [citation:6].

📋 Key Planning Principle: External modifications to listed buildings and conservation areas almost always require planning permission or listed building consent. Internal installations that do not alter the external appearance do not require consent [citation:1][citation:6].

Case Studies – What the Planning System Allows

Internal Screens – Accepted

North Yorkshire Council's shop front security guidance explicitly states: "The use of internal grilles does not require planning permission. Internal grilles can be installed immediately behind the windows" [citation:6]. The guidance further notes that "internal 'glass' or 'see-through transparent' screens will generally be acceptable" [citation:6].

This principle applies directly to mosquito screens: an internal, retractable screen installed behind the window glass does not alter the external appearance and does not require planning permission [citation:6].

External Screens – Require Consent

By contrast, external grilles and screens face stricter scrutiny. North Yorkshire Council advises that "all external grilles require planning permission" [citation:6]. For listed buildings and conservation areas, "external roller grilles may be acceptable subject to stringent design criteria" — but are "unacceptable on listed buildings and in conservation areas, main shopping streets and district or local shopping centres" [citation:6].

Similarly, the Woodbridge case illustrates the consequences of installing external protective bars without listed building consent: the council ordered their removal [citation:1].

Polycarbonate and Protective Screens – Need Careful Design

Protective screens, including polycarbonate screens, can be acceptable if carefully designed. In Lerwick, Shetland, listed building consent was granted for "new polycarbonate protective screens" as part of a broader window replacement project at St Margaret's, a listed church [citation:5]. However, this was part of a full listed building consent application, with detailed drawings and specifications [citation:5].

CJL Designs, a specialist window restoration studio, offers "window protection grilles and polycarbonate screens" for listed buildings and conservation areas, demonstrating that protective screens can be acceptable when designed sympathetically and installed with appropriate approvals [citation:9].

🔑 The Lesson: External screens require planning permission or listed building consent. Internal screens do not. By choosing an internal solution like the H01, homeowners avoid the planning minefield entirely [citation:6].

Heritage Windows and Mosquito Protection – The Challenge

Historic UK windows pose unique challenges for mosquito protection:

  • Sash windows — The sliding mechanism means traditional fixed screens interfere with operation. The H01's retractable design allows full sash movement.
  • Casement windows — Inward or outward opening casements require screens that don't block the opening. The H01 mounts on the interior frame, preserving full opening.
  • Arched and shaped windows — Many historic buildings have arched, curved, or irregularly shaped windows. The H01 is custom-made to any shape or size [citation:3].
  • Stone mullion windows — Popular in historic buildings, these require slim sightlines. Specialist heritage glazing systems are designed to meet these requirements [citation:3].

Traditional screen solutions — fixed mesh panels, external grilles — often fail on heritage properties because they damage historic fabric, alter external appearance, or require planning consent. The H01 solves all three problems.
