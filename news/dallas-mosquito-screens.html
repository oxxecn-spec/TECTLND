Dallas – America's #8 Mosquito City

Dallas ranked #8 on Orkin's 2026 Mosquito Cities List, down one spot from #7 in 2025 [citation:13]. The ranking is based on the number of new residential mosquito treatments performed by pest control professionals over a one-year period, reflecting where pest control demand is highest [citation:13].

Orkin entomologist Shannon Sked explained: "Over the past decade, we've seen mosquito activity expand beyond traditional hotspots," with climate change creating conditions for the yellow fever mosquito to thrive [citation:13]. Dallas's position in the top 10 reflects the consistent and severe mosquito pressure that defines North Texas summers.

📊 Orkin 2026 Top 10 Mosquito Cities: 1. Los Angeles, 2. Chicago, 3. New York, 4. Detroit, 5. Atlanta, 6. Washington DC, 7. Houston, 8. Dallas, 9. Cleveland, 10. Denver [citation:13]

West Nile Virus – Dallas's Urban Threat

West Nile virus is the most common mosquito-borne disease in Dallas. The primary vector is the Culex quinquefasciatus mosquito, also known as the Southern house mosquito [citation:3][citation:8].

2012 Park Cities Outbreak – A Historic Warning

In 2012, West Nile virus "burned through the Park Cities," two wealthy municipalities surrounded by Dallas, causing 225 cases of West Nile fever, 173 cases of the more serious neuroinvasive form of the disease, and 19 deaths [citation:3][citation:8].

Dr. Robert Haley, an infectious disease expert at UT Southwestern, explained that urban heat islands were a key factor: "The downtown area is what's called a heat island. It's always two, three, or four degrees warmer in downtown Dallas than in outlying areas. And the prevailing winds move that hot air up into Park Cities, making it even warmer" [citation:3].

2025-2026 West Nile Activity

  • August 2026: Dallas County reported its first human case of West Nile virus for the season. The patient was a Dallas resident in their early 50s diagnosed with the neuroinvasive form of the disease [citation:2].
  • 2026 Trap Data: There were 167 positive mosquito samples in Dallas County as of August 2026 [citation:2].
  • 2025: Dallas County reported 10 human cases of West Nile virus with one death — a 70-year-old man with underlying medical conditions diagnosed with West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease [citation:6][citation:11][citation:15].
  • 2025 Trap Data: Dallas County found 245 positive traps and reported 8 human cases as of October 2025 [citation:11].
  • 2024: Dallas County reported one West Nile death in North Dallas [citation:9].

Dr. Philip Huang, Director of Dallas County Health and Human Services, warned: "Our first human case is an important reminder that West Nile virus is still active here in Dallas County. Take a few minutes to protect yourself from mosquito bites, especially when spending time outdoors" [citation:2].

🦟 Culex quinquefasciatus — The Southern House Mosquito: Dr. Robert Haley explains: "The Culex quinquefasciatus lives around houses — they breed in bottle caps full of water, drains, overwatered lawns, and any kind of anything holding water around your house — and they don't go very far." This means your own yard is the primary source of risk — making window screens essential [citation:3][citation:8].

Urban Heat Island Effect – Why Dallas Is a Hotspot

Dallas's urban heat island effect is a major driver of mosquito-borne disease risk. The Culex quinquefasciatus mosquito thrives when temperatures reach about 80°F, and "in warmer weather, mosquitoes bite more" [citation:10].

As Dr. Haley explained, "urban outbreaks, not rural outbreaks" are the pattern for West Nile virus in Dallas [citation:3]. The combination of:

  • Concrete and pavement — absorb and retain heat, creating warmer nighttime temperatures
  • Dense housing — provides abundant human hosts for mosquitoes
  • Drainage systems — create standing water habitats
  • Overwatered lawns — provide breeding sites for Culex mosquitoes

creates a perfect environment for mosquito-borne disease transmission. This is why the Park Cities — just north of downtown Dallas — experienced such a devastating outbreak in 2012 [citation:3][citation:8].

Aedes Mosquitoes – Dengue, Zika & Chikungunya Risk

Beyond West Nile virus, Dallas faces growing risks from Aedes mosquitoes. A 2025 NIH-funded study of the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex found that:

  • Aedes aegypti — the primary vector for dengue, Zika, and chikungunya — is positively correlated with hot and dry conditions within densely populated locations, with peak abundance from weeks 33 to 44 (mid-August through early November) [citation:14].
  • Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito) is positively correlated with cooler temperatures in higher socioeconomic locations with lower human population density, with peak abundance from weeks 19 to 32 (early May through early August) [citation:14].
  • The diversity of environmental conditions in the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex enables both species to "exploit differential niche spaces," increasing overall disease risk [citation:14].

This means that Dallas homeowners face multiple mosquito species with different seasonal peaks and different disease risks — making comprehensive protection essential.

🧬 DFW Aedes Study: A 2025 NIH-funded study of the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex found that Aedes aegypti (dengue/Zika vector) peaks in late summer/fall, while Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito) peaks in late spring/early summer. The diversity of environmental conditions in DFW enables both species to exploit different niches [citation:14].

Dallas Mosquito Season – May Through November

Dallas's mosquito season is long and intense. According to Dallas County Health and Human Services:

  • Active season: May 1 through October 31, with monitoring running through mid-November [citation:4][citation:11]
  • Peak season: June through August, when temperatures climb into the mid-90s and humidity stays high, shortening the mosquito life cycle to 5-7 days [citation:5]
  • Warm, wet winters (like 2025's January rainfall of 5.69 inches) lead to amplification of the virus early in the summer [citation:9][citation:3]
  • Heavy rainfall — Dallas had nearly 5 inches of rain in May 2025 — creates standing water, and mosquitoes can grow from egg to adult in as little as seven days [citation:9]

The City of Dallas maintains an integrated mosquito management program with:

  • 90 trap site locations citywide for West Nile virus monitoring
  • Spraying operations activated within 24 hours of a positive trap result, within a ½-mile radius
  • Larvicide treatments to reduce mosquito populations
  • Community outreach and education [citation:4]

But government spraying is not 100% effective — personal protection, including window screens, is essential [citation:4].
