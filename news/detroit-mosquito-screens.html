Detroit – America's #4 Mosquito City

Detroit has consistently ranked among the top mosquito cities in the US. In Orkin's 2026 list, Detroit moved up to the #4 position, behind Los Angeles (#1), Chicago (#2), and New York (#3), and ahead of Atlanta (#5) [citation:1][citation:6][citation:11].

The ranking is based on the number of new residential mosquito treatments performed by pest control professionals over a one-year period from March 2025 to March 2026 [citation:1][citation:9]. The Vacant Lot Problem – Detroit's Unique Mosquito Challenge

Detroit's mosquito problem is deeply connected to its urban landscape. The city has a significant number of vacant lots and abandoned structures scattered throughout its neighborhoods — a legacy of decades of population decline [citation:8]. These unmanaged sites create meaningful pest pressure for the occupied homes around them.

According to a local pest control analysis, "vacant lots accumulate standing water from rainfall that becomes mosquito breeding habitat — a single neglected site can generate significant mosquito pressure for an entire block radius" [citation:8]. Abandoned swimming pools, neglected birdbaths, waterlogged flowerpots, and dirty gutters all become havens for mosquitoes [citation:3][citation:13].

As entomologist Mark Sheperdigian explained during the 2009 economic downturn: "You've got all these houses in foreclosure or abandoned all over. And abandoned swimming pools? They get pretty green and can breed mosquitoes by the hundredweight" [citation:3]. Today, that challenge persists.

Detroit's vacant lot situation is more pronounced than Chicago's, creating neighborhood-level mosquito pressure that Chicago's more uniformly developed urban fabric doesn't experience to the same degree [citation:8]. This means that even if your own property is well-maintained, a vacant lot nearby can still generate significant mosquito pressure for your home.

West Nile Virus – A Growing Threat in Detroit

West Nile virus is the most common mosquito-borne disease in the continental US, and Detroit is at the center of the threat [citation:4].

West Nile in Detroit: By the Numbers

  • 2026: Wayne County mosquito pool tested positive for West Nile in July — the first positive sample in the county and the 13th in Michigan [citation:12]
  • 2025: Michigan reported 61 human cases of West Nile virus, up from 34 in 2024 [citation:2]
  • 2024: Michigan reported 49 cases (CDC data), including one human case in Detroit [citation:2][citation:12]
  • 2023: Michigan had 26 cases [citation:2]
  • 2024: 102 mosquito pools, 25 birds, and one horse in Michigan tested positive for West Nile virus [citation:7]
  • 2024: A dead falcon found near Seven Mile and the Southfield Freeway tested positive for West Nile virus [citation:7]

The West Nile virus originated in central Africa and appeared in New York City around 1999. Now, it is present in Michigan, and its primary carrier is the northern house mosquito (Culex pipiens) [citation:4].

Climate Change Extends the Threat

Mosquito season in Michigan is lengthening due to climate change. MSU Professor Ned Walker explains that "seasonality is not a static thing, it's changing and dynamic, and we can have biting mosquitoes as long as the weather is warm enough" — and temperatures of 50°F or higher are sufficient for mosquitoes to fly [citation:4].

Walker and his team have found that mosquitoes carrying West Nile virus have been active for longer periods, lasting into October when previously their activity would have stopped a month earlier. Continued blood feeding activity into the fall means a greater potential transmission period for the virus [citation:4].

Outbreaks more commonly occur in years that are especially hot and dry — and 2025 was one of those years [citation:4].

🦟 West Nile Virus Symptoms: Most people infected with West Nile don't develop symptoms, but about 20% experience fever, head and body aches, joint pain, vomiting, diarrhea, or a rash. Symptoms develop 2 to 14 days after the patient is bitten. Older people, individuals with weakened immune systems, or those with chronic illness can suffer from potentially deadly issues such as meningitis or brain infection [citation:12].

The Role of Abandoned Structures in Mosquito Breeding

Detroit's abandoned structures are more than just an eyesore — they're a public health hazard when it comes to mosquitoes. A single abandoned home can have:

  • Open foundations with standing water
  • Clogged gutters that collect rainwater
  • Old tires, buckets, and containers that hold water
  • Leaky basements and crawl spaces
  • Abandoned swimming pools and hot tubs that collect stagnant water [citation:3][citation:8][citation:13]

As one Detroit resident reported via the city's SeeClickFix system, an overgrown area behind a garage with a full-sized hot tub collecting water "has become a breeding ground for mosquitoes. The neighbors can't even enjoy an evening outside without being chased back inside by hundreds of mosquitoes" [citation:13].

Homeowners near vacant lots or abandoned structures should establish more frequent perimeter monitoring given the elevated mosquito pressure from adjacent unmanaged sites [citation:8].
