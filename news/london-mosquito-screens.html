LSHTM Modelling – London's 2060s Dengue Risk

In 2026, the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine published updated climate modelling for dengue transmission in the UK . The findings are stark: by the 2060s, the climate in London and surrounding areas will likely support endemic (sustained local) transmission of dengue .

The modelling accounts for multiple factors:

  • Rising average temperatures in London, with summers becoming hotter and longer
  • Milder winters that allow mosquito species to survive and breed year-round
  • Increased rainfall creating more standing water habitats
  • The urban heat island effect — London's built environment retains heat, creating microclimates warmer than surrounding areas

LSHTM modelling also shows that the Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus) — a known dengue vector — may already be capable of surviving summer seasons in London, with the potential for full overwintering by the 2040s .

London's Historic Mosquito Connection

London has a long history with mosquito-borne diseases. The UK formerly had endemic malaria — known as "marsh fever" or "ague" — with the last major outbreak occurring in 1917-1921 . The last known indigenous case of malaria in the UK was diagnosed in 1957 .

The malaria parasite was carried by Anopheles atroparvus mosquitoes, which were once widespread in the UK, especially in coastal marshes and wetlands . While malaria was eventually eradicated, its historical presence demonstrates that the UK has both the right mosquito species and the right ecological conditions to support mosquito-borne disease transmission.

With climate change, there is renewed concern that warmer temperatures could make the UK suitable once again for malaria transmission . This historical precedent, combined with modern climate change projections, underscores the importance of mosquito protection in London and throughout the UK.

Heathrow Airport – A Gateway for Invasive Mosquitoes

In September 2023, UKHSA researchers made a concerning discovery: Aedes aegypti eggs were detected at a freight storage facility near London Heathrow Airport . The only previous known incursion of this species in the UK was an adult male discovered near Liverpool in 2017 .

Aedes aegypti — the yellow fever mosquito — is the primary vector for dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and yellow fever. It is an aggressive daytime biter that prefers feeding on humans and breeds in small containers of water .

The detection at Heathrow highlights the role of international travel and trade in introducing invasive mosquito species to the UK. Heathrow is one of the world's busiest airports, with thousands of flights arriving from countries where Aedes aegypti is endemic .

UKHSA now conducts surveillance at 117 locations across England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, including seaports, airports, and roadside locations .

London's Urban Heat Island – A Mosquito Accelerator

London's urban heat island effect is a major factor in the city's mosquito vulnerability. The built environment — concrete, asphalt, and brick — absorbs and retains heat, making London consistently warmer than surrounding rural areas .

This creates ideal conditions for mosquito reproduction:

  • Warmer temperatures accelerate mosquito development from egg to adult
  • Milder nights extend mosquito activity periods
  • Longer seasons allow multiple generations of mosquitoes per year
  • Protected microclimates in urban areas allow species to survive that would not survive in rural areas

The combination of the urban heat island effect and climate change projections makes London particularly vulnerable to the establishment of invasive mosquito species .
