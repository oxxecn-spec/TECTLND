Los Angeles – America's #1 Mosquito City

For the sixth consecutive year, Los Angeles has been ranked the worst city in the United States for mosquito activity. According to Orkin's 2026 Mosquito Cities List, LA maintained its top spot, with Chicago and New York following at #2 and #3[citation:11].

This ranking reflects a significant geographic shift in mosquito activity. From 2015 through 2020, Atlanta held the top spot. But since 2021, Los Angeles has consistently ranked #1 — marking a major shift in mosquito pressure from the Southeast to the West Coast[citation:11]. As Orkin entomologist Shannon Sked explained, "Over the past decade, we've seen mosquito activity expand beyond traditional hotspots and become a nationwide concern"[citation:8].

📊 Orkin 2026 Top 10 Mosquito Cities: 1. Los Angeles, 2. Chicago, 3. New York, 4. Detroit, 5. Atlanta, 6. Washington DC, 7. Houston, 8. Dallas, 9. Cleveland, 10. Denver[citation:11]

Why LA Is a Mosquito Hotspot

🌆 The Urban Heat Island Effect

Los Angeles's sprawling urban landscape creates a powerful "urban heat island" effect — cities are consistently warmer than surrounding rural areas due to concrete, asphalt, and lack of vegetation. In Dallas, which has a similar urban heat island effect to LA, researchers found that the downtown area is "always two, three, or four degrees warmer" than outlying areas, creating ideal conditions for mosquito-borne disease transmission[citation:7].

The Culex quinquefasciatus mosquito, which spreads West Nile virus, thrives in urban environments. It "lives around houses — they breed in bottle caps full of water, drains, overwatered lawns, and any kind of anything holding water around your house — and they don't go very far"[citation:7]. This makes LA homes particularly vulnerable.

🦟 Multiple Mosquito Species

Los Angeles is home to multiple disease-carrying mosquito species:

  • Culex mosquitoes — spread West Nile virus, the most common mosquito-borne disease in the US[citation:1]. In 2024, Fresno County (Central California) saw West Nile cases and a death[citation:6].
  • Aedes mosquitoes — including the Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus) and the yellow fever mosquito (Aedes aegypti), which spread dengue, Zika, and chikungunya. Aedes mosquitoes are now established throughout Southern California[citation:9].
  • Southern house mosquito — the primary vector for West Nile virus in urban areas[citation:7].

In Southern California, mosquitoes carrying West Nile virus have been detected in multiple cities including Westminster, Costa Mesa, Newport Beach, Buena Park, Garden Grove, Santa Ana, Seal Beach, Huntington Beach, Cypress, and Fullerton[citation:9].

🌡️ Climate Change and Mosquito Disease Days

According to a Climate Central analysis of 239 major US cities, 95% of cities experienced an increase in mosquito disease days — days with temperatures suitable for West Nile virus transmission. On average, US cities now experience 18 more mosquito disease days annually than in the early 1970s[citation:1].

In California, San Francisco saw the largest increase of any US city — 52 more mosquito disease days. Santa Maria (+49) and Salinas (+40) also ranked in the top five[citation:1]. As climate change continues to lengthen mosquito seasons, LA homeowners face a growing threat.

Mosquito-Borne Disease Risks in LA

West Nile Virus

West Nile virus is the most common mosquito-borne disease in the US, with about 2,000 people diagnosed annually and cases typically peaking in August and September[citation:1]. While about 80% of infected people show no symptoms, about one in five develop flu-like symptoms, and about one in 150 develop severe neurological illness such as encephalitis or meningitis[citation:7].

Aedes-Borne Diseases

With the establishment of Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus in Southern California, LA faces additional risks:

  • Dengue — California reported 720 dengue cases in 2024, making it the second-highest state for dengue incidence after Florida, with 18 locally acquired cases across three counties.
  • Zika — remains a threat, with ongoing transmission in the Americas and imported cases in the US.
  • Chikungunya — has spread to more than 110 countries globally.

🌍 Global Mosquito Toll: The World Health Organization estimates that over 700,000 people die from mosquito-borne diseases each year. Malaria alone accounts for more than 600,000 deaths annually, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa. Dengue cases have risen dramatically, with over 5 million reported in 2023. The global burden underscores the importance of personal protection — and the H01 is your first line of defense at home.
