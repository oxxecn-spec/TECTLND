The Rising Threat of Mosquito-Borne Diseases in America

Mosquito-borne diseases were once considered a tropical problem. Not anymore. Climate change, international travel, and expanding mosquito habitats have made them a nationwide concern. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports that diseases transmitted by mosquitoes, ticks, and fleas have tripled in the US between 2004 and 2016.

West Nile Virus – The Most Common in the US

West Nile virus is the leading cause of mosquito-borne disease in the continental United States. It was first detected in the US in 1999 and has since spread to all 48 contiguous states. According to the CDC, over 2,000 cases are reported annually, with hundreds of deaths since 1999. Most cases occur between June and September, when mosquitoes are most active.

While 80% of infected people show no symptoms, the remaining 20% can experience fever, headache, body aches, and fatigue. About 1 in 150 cases develop severe neurological illness, including encephalitis or meningitis, which can be fatal.

Zika Virus – A Birth Defect Risk

Zika virus gained global attention during the 2015-2016 outbreak in the Americas. In the US, local transmission has been reported in Florida, Texas, and Hawaii. While symptoms are usually mild, Zika is particularly dangerous for pregnant women, as it can cause microcephaly and other severe birth defects.

The CDC continues to monitor Zika activity, and travelers returning from affected areas can introduce the virus to local mosquito populations, creating a cycle of local transmission.

Dengue – Returning to the US Mainland

Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral infection that causes flu-like symptoms and, in severe cases, can develop into life-threatening dengue hemorrhagic fever. Before 1970, only nine countries had experienced severe dengue epidemics. Today, the disease is endemic in more than 100 countries, including parts of the US. Florida, Texas, and Hawaii have all reported locally acquired dengue cases in recent years.

Eastern Equine Encephalitis (EEE) – Rare but Deadly

EEE is one of the most severe mosquito-borne diseases in the US. Though rare, with only about 5-10 cases reported annually, it has a mortality rate of about 30%. Survivors often suffer from permanent neurological damage. EEE is most common along the Eastern and Gulf Coast states, particularly in Massachusetts, New York, and Florida.

Malaria – A Global Threat on US Soil

While malaria was declared eliminated in the US in 1951, the country still sees about 2,000 cases annually, mostly from travelers returning from endemic regions. However, local transmission – known as "airport malaria" – has occurred near international airports. In 2023, the CDC issued an alert after locally acquired malaria cases were reported in Florida, Texas, and Maryland for the first time in 20 years.

🌍 Global Mosquito Toll: The World Health Organization estimates that over 700,000 people die from mosquito-borne diseases each year. Malaria alone accounts for more than 600,000 deaths annually, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa. Dengue cases have risen dramatically, with over 5 million reported in 2023. The global burden of mosquito-borne diseases underscores the importance of personal protection – and the H01 is your first line of defense at home.
