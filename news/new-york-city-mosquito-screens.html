NYC – America's #3 Mosquito City

New York City has consistently ranked among the top mosquito cities in the US. In Orkin's 2026 list, NYC held the #3 position, ahead of Detroit (#4) and Atlanta (#5), behind only Los Angeles (#1) and Chicago (#2) [citation:8][citation:9].

NYC's mosquito problem is driven by its dense urban environment, extensive parklands, and the 5.2 million catch basins across the city that collect standing water — perfect mosquito breeding grounds [citation:2][citation:5]. The NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (DOHMH) conducts year-round mosquito surveillance, trapping mosquitoes at over 260 locations and testing them for diseases [citation:2][citation:5].

🦟 NYC Mosquito Surveillance: The DOHMH monitors mosquito populations across all five boroughs, testing adult mosquitoes for West Nile and other viruses at the Public Health Laboratory. If WNV is detected, supplemental traps are added to better define virus distribution [citation:5].

West Nile Virus – NYC's #1 Mosquito-Borne Threat

West Nile virus (WNV) first emerged in the United States in 1999, with New York City at the epicenter of the outbreak [citation:9][citation:12]. Since then, WNV has become endemic in NYC, with cases reported every year [citation:6][citation:9].

Key WNV facts for NYC:

  • Season length: The WNV transmission season in New York State has lengthened by an average of 20 days over the past 25 years, beginning 3.8 days earlier and ending 16.3 days later [citation:9][citation:12].
  • Annual cases: Since 1999, there have been between 2 and 45 people with West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease annually. Six of the nine years with more than 25 cases have been since 2010 [citation:6].
  • Climate change link: Climate model analyses indicate that the observed increase in WNV season length is 6.4 times more likely under historical climate forcing than under pre-industrial conditions [citation:9][citation:12].
  • High-risk populations: People over 60 have the highest risk of severe WNV illness. Most infections occur due to a mosquito bite within NYC [citation:6].
  • Vector species: The primary WNV vector in NYC is the Culex pipiens mosquito, which breeds in stagnant water in catch basins, tires, birdbaths, and clogged gutters [citation:2][citation:5].

The NYC Health Department's West Nile Virus Plan for 2025 recommends that residents "make sure doors and windows have tight-fitting screens. Repair or replace screens that have tears or holes" [citation:2].

🌡️ Climate Change & WNV Season: A 2025 study published in bioRxiv found that the WNV transmission season in NYS has lengthened by an average of 20 days since 1999. Longer transmission seasons are positively associated with higher WNV prevalence in both mosquito vectors and humans [citation:9].

Aedes Mosquitoes – Dengue, Zika & Chikungunya Risk

Beyond West Nile virus, NYC faces growing risks from Aedes mosquitoes — the species that spread dengue, Zika, and chikungunya. A 2019-2020 study of mosquitoes in NYC identified Culex pipiens, Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito), and Drosophila relpelta — all considered invasive species [citation:8].

The Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito) is now established in NYC, and is known to be a vector of West Nile virus, Western Equine Encephalitis, and Japanese Encephalitis virus [citation:8]. It also transmits dengue and chikungunya, which have been reported in NYC among travelers.

In addition, Brooklyn community gardens have been identified as breeding sites for both Aedes albopictus and Culex pipiens, as both species breed in human-made plastic containers. Community garden abundance is a concern for urban mosquito management [citation:11].

NYC Landlord Screen Requirements – What Renters Need to Know

If you rent in NYC, you need to know your rights — and your responsibilities. Here's what the law says:

  • Landlords are NOT required to provide screens under the Housing Maintenance Code [citation:10]. Real estate attorney Catharine Grad confirms: "The Housing Maintenance Code sets out requirements for housing in New York City, and it does not require that a landlord maintain storm windows or screens in windows" [citation:10].
  • If you're rent-stabilized and screens were provided at move-in, the landlord must maintain them as a "required service" [citation:10].
  • Window guards ARE required if the building has more than three apartments and children 10 or younger reside there [citation:10].
  • Natural ventilation requirements: The NYC Building Code requires naturally ventilated buildings to provide a minimum openable area equivalent to 5% of floor area [citation:4]. However, window stops installed for child safety can limit this.

The NYC Health Department emphasizes: "Make sure doors and windows have tight-fitting screens. Repair or replace screens that have tears or holes" [citation:2]. But for NYC renters, this responsibility falls on the tenant, not the landlord [citation:10].

NYC Building Code – What It Says About Screens

While NYC's Housing Maintenance Code doesn't require landlords to provide screens, building owners must maintain vermin and insect protection for certain building systems [citation:7].

Specifically, the NYC Plumbing Code requires that "inlets and vents to the system shall be inspected to verify that each is protected to prevent the entrance of insects and vermin into the storage..." [citation:7]. This applies to nonpotable water systems, not residential windows [citation:7].

For residential windows, the International Property Maintenance Code, adopted by NYC, requires screens for "all doors, windows and other outside openings in residential buildings" [citation:4]. However, enforcement varies, and it is generally the tenant's responsibility to ensure their own windows are screened.

For NYC Health Code, the DOHMH's West Nile Virus Plan recommends screens but does not mandate them [citation:5].

The bottom line: If you want window screens in NYC, you'll likely need to provide them yourself [citation:10].
