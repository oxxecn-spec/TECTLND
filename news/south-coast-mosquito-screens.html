South Coast – The UK's Gateway for Invasive Mosquitoes

The south coast's geography makes it the primary entry point for invasive mosquito species into the UK [citation:11]. The region has:

  • Dover, Southampton, Portsmouth, Folkestone — Major ports handling millions of passengers and vehicles annually from continental Europe, where Aedes albopictus is already established across parts of southern and central Europe [citation:3][citation:7]
  • London Heathrow, Gatwick, Southampton airports — Handling international flights from regions where Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are endemic [citation:3]
  • M20, M3, M27 motorways — Major transport corridors connecting ports to the rest of the UK [citation:11]
  • Proximity to France — The Asian tiger mosquito is already established in northern France, including areas around Calais [citation:10]

UKHSA has identified the south coast as a high-priority surveillance area, with enhanced monitoring at ports, airports, and motorway service stations [citation:11]. UKHSA has also been running a Nationwide Mosquito Programme since 2010, at over 70 wetland sites across the UK, including coastal wetlands on the south coast [citation:5].

UKHSA Surveillance – What's Been Detected

UKHSA's invasive mosquito surveillance has been running since 2010, with ovitraps (water-filled traps designed to attract egg-laying female mosquitoes) at 117 locations across England, Wales and Northern Ireland — including seaports, airports, and roadside locations [citation:3][citation:11]. The traps are checked biweekly from June to October, when mosquito activity is highest [citation:11].

Key Detections on the South Coast

  • September 2023: Aedes aegypti eggs (Egyptian/yellow fever mosquito) were detected at a freight storage facility near London Heathrow Airport. This was the first ever detection of Aedes aegypti eggs in the UK [citation:3][citation:7]. The only previous known incursion was an adult male discovered near Liverpool in 2017 [citation:6].
  • August 2024: Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito) was detected near a fast-food drive-through at a service station on the M20 motorway in Kent — the first detection since 2019 [citation:3][citation:7]. Researchers hypothesised that the mosquito "may have arrived via a vehicle from mainland Europe and exited when the vehicle stopped" [citation:3].
  • 2016-2019: Aedes albopictus eggs were detected at a truck stop in Kent, with enhanced surveillance preventing establishment [citation:11].
  • 2017: An adult male Aedes aegypti was discovered near Liverpool — the only previous confirmed finding of that species in the UK [citation:6].

Critically, no additional eggs, larvae, or adults were found in other traps during routine and enhanced surveillance, suggesting these were transient incursions that were successfully contained [citation:3][citation:7]. However, UKHSA's lead author Colin Johnston emphasised: "What stood out during this surveillance was how dynamic and responsive our work needs to be. Each detection triggered enhanced local surveillance and control measures" [citation:7].

🔬 2023-2024 Detections: "In September 2023, Aedes aegypti eggs were detected near London Heathrow Airport — the first ever detection of this species in the UK. In August 2024, Aedes albopictus was detected at a service station on the M20 in Kent — the first detection since 2019. Both were isolated incursions, successfully contained" [citation:3][citation:7].

Asian Tiger Mosquito – The Greatest Threat

The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus) is one of the most invasive and dangerous mosquito species in the world. It has now established populations across parts of southern and central Europe, demonstrating its ability to survive in temperate climates [citation:7].

Why It's a Threat

  • Aggressive daytime biter — Unlike most mosquitoes that bite at dawn and dusk, the Asian tiger mosquito bites all day long [citation:10]
  • Disease vector — It can transmit dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and West Nile virus [citation:3][citation:7]
  • Breeds in small containers — It breeds in birdbaths, tyres, plant saucers, bottle caps, and children's toys — making urban and suburban environments ideal habitat [citation:11]
  • Readily transported by vehicles — It travels easily in vehicles, making motorway service stations high-risk locations for detection [citation:3][citation:11]

In Europe, Aedes albopictus has contributed to local outbreaks of dengue in Spain, France, and Italy [citation:11]. Preventing its establishment in the UK requires coordinated surveillance, early detection, and rapid control measures [citation:11].

Native Mosquitoes — Culex modestus and West Nile Risk

Beyond invasive species, the south coast is home to Culex modestus, a native mosquito species known to carry West Nile virus. This species was last recorded in the UK in 1937 but reappeared in 2010, with numbers steadily increasing and now established in Essex and Kent [citation:12].

UKHSA's Nationwide Mosquito Programme, which has been running since 2010, monitors native mosquito populations at over 70 wetland sites across the UK, including coastal wetlands on the south coast [citation:5]. The programme aims to better understand the seasonality and distribution of native mosquitoes and detect new species. Native mosquitoes can pose a public health risk, with some UK species known to be competent vectors for Usutu virus — which has recently been detected in mosquito and bird populations in the UK — and West Nile Virus [citation:5].

In 2025, the BMJ reported that West Nile virus fragments were detected in UK mosquitoes for the first time — from a wetland area in Nottinghamshire [citation:12]. This serves as a wake-up call for south coast residents.

Additionally, native mosquitoes such as Aedes vexans and Aedes caspius are prolific human biters, leading to nuisance biting complaints where they occur — particularly in coastal areas and wetlands [citation:5].

🦟 Culex modestus in the South Coast: Culex modestus, a known West Nile virus vector, is now established in Essex and Kent after an 80-year absence. UKHSA's wetland surveillance monitors native mosquito populations across the south coast [citation:5][citation:12].

Salt-Marsh Mosquitoes — Aedes detritus on the Wirral

Research is also being conducted on Aedes detritus, a salt-marsh mosquito that is a significant biting nuisance in the Neston area next to the Dee Estuary, UK — near the south coast [citation:5]. The Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine is currently studying the ecology of Aedes detritus to improve understanding of its dispersal patterns [citation:5].

Fieldwork is investigating the abundance and diversity of mosquito vectors within different land uses on Wirral and the dispersal patterns of Aedes detritus from breeding sites in the salt marshes. BG-Pro traps will be placed along transects extending 8 km (5 miles) inland from the salt marsh [citation:5].

This research "will provide valuable insights into the ecology of Ae. detritus, informing vector control strategies in UK coastal regions and enhancing our understanding of its potential to spread emerging mosquito-borne diseases" [citation:5].

Climate Change – Accelerating the Threat

Climate change is increasing the suitability of habitat for invasive mosquito species in the UK [citation:7]. Rising temperatures and milder winters may increase northward incursions of invasive species, putting new populations at risk of disease transmission [citation:7].

Aedes aegypti, previously considered unsuitable for establishment at northern latitudes, may now find conditions more favourable [citation:6]. As one study noted, "Northerly latitudes have previously been considered unsuitable for the establishment of Aedes aegypti, but this study demonstrates the need for surveillance and vigilance in countries believed to be climatically unsuitable" [citation:6].

The south coast is particularly vulnerable — it has both the warmest climate in the UK and the highest risk of incursion from continental Europe [citation:11].

South Coast Towns – At Risk

South coast towns face the highest risk of mosquito incursion:

  • Kent (Dover, Folkestone, Maidstone, Canterbury) — The frontline, with the M20 detection and proximity to France [citation:3][citation:11]
  • Sussex (Brighton, Eastbourne, Hastings) — Coastal towns with high humidity and tourist traffic
  • Hampshire (Southampton, Portsmouth, Bournemouth) — Major ports and ferry terminals
  • Surrey & Sussex — Towns near Gatwick Airport
  • Cornwall & Devon — Coastal areas with mild winters and high tourist traffic
