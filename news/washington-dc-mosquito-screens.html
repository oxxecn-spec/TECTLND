Washington DC – America's #6 Mosquito City

Washington DC has moved up two spots in Orkin's 2026 Mosquito Cities List, ranking #6 nationally [citation:4]. The ranking is based on the number of new residential mosquito treatments performed by Orkin from March 18, 2025, to March 18, 2026, reflecting where pest control demand is highest [citation:4].

Orkin entomologist Shannon Sked explained: "Over the past decade, we've seen mosquito activity expand beyond traditional hotspots and become a nationwide concern" [citation:4]. DC's rise in the rankings reflects the growing mosquito pressure in the Mid-Atlantic region, driven by climate conditions and the expanding range of invasive mosquito species [citation:4].

West Nile Virus – An Active Threat in DC

West Nile virus has been endemic in Washington DC since 2002 and remains the only mosquito-borne disease endemic in the District [citation:5].

2025 West Nile Detection in DC

  • July 2025: Four American crows tested positive for West Nile virus in four different DC wards — Wards 2, 3, 4, and 5 — marking the first detections in wild birds since 2021 [citation:5].
  • 2025 Mosquito Pools: As of August 2025, 3 mosquito pools (1.3%) had tested positive for West Nile virus, collected at the end of June [citation:5].
  • 2024 Mosquito Pools: A total of 14 mosquito pools (4.0%) tested positive for West Nile virus in 2024 [citation:5].
  • Human Cases: Between 2020 and 2024, an average of 3 human West Nile virus cases were reported annually in DC, all between July and September. Five cases were reported in 2024, including one asymptomatic blood donor [citation:5].

The DC Department of Health warns that West Nile virus is transmitted primarily by infected Culex species mosquitoes, which "commonly circulates between birds and mosquitoes" and can spill over into humans [citation:5]. About 80% of infected people show no symptoms, but less than 1% develop severe neuroinvasive disease, which can be life-threatening. Those over 50 and immunocompromised individuals are at higher risk for severe illness [citation:5].

Healthcare providers in DC are advised to consider West Nile virus in patients with compatible symptoms, as it can share similar symptoms with other arboviral diseases such as dengue and chikungunya [citation:5].

In July 2025, four American crows tested positive across four wards, and 3 mosquito pools (1.3%) were positive. The virus is transmitted by infected Culex mosquitoes that feed on infected birds and then bite people [citation:5].

Dengue Fever – A Growing Threat in the DC Region

In 2025, the first-ever case of locally transmitted dengue fever was confirmed in Virginia, in a patient living in the Fairfax and Loudoun counties — the DC suburbs [citation:13]. The patient had not recently left Virginia but had been in close contact with an international traveler who was diagnosed with the same virus. Health officials believe a local mosquito bit the infected traveler and then the new patient, spreading the virus locally [citation:13].

Virginia is now the ninth state to report local dengue transmission since 2010, joining Florida, Hawaii, Texas, California, Arizona, North Carolina, West Virginia, and New York [citation:13]. Nationwide, 3,798 dengue cases were reported in 2024, a 359% increase from the annual average of 828 cases between 2010 and 2023. Among these, 105 were locally acquired [citation:10].

Dengue is spread through the bites of infected Aedes species mosquitoes, including Aedes albopictus (Asian tiger mosquito) and Aedes aegypti [citation:13]. The Asian tiger mosquito is established in the DC area [citation:2]. Symptoms include fever, severe headache, muscle and joint aches, and nausea. Severe dengue can cause internal bleeding and even death if not treated promptly [citation:13].

Health officials recommend that travelers heading to dengue-endemic regions wear insect repellent, long-sleeved shirts, and long pants, and sleep in air-conditioned rooms or rooms with window screens [citation:13].

Invasive Plants – A Hidden Driver of DC's Mosquito Problem

Recent studies have identified an unexpected factor contributing to DC's mosquito pressure: invasive plants. Research shows that invasive trees in economically disadvantaged neighborhoods of Baltimore and Washington DC are contributing to a rise in mosquito populations [citation:7].

The nonnative leaves serve as an excellent nourishment source for mosquito larvae, promoting faster growth and larger size, which ultimately enhances their ability to transmit diseases. This finding underscores the significance of plant quality over quantity in managing mosquito outbreaks [citation:7].

The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus) is listed as a main alien, invasive species in the Washington DC Area [citation:2]. In addition, invasive plants like kudzu and Japanese knotweed — common in the DC region — create dense, moist undergrowth that provides ideal mosquito resting habitat.

This means that neighborhood vegetation can quietly shape public health risks — and even if you maintain your own yard, adjacent green spaces with invasive plants may contribute to mosquito pressure [citation:7].

The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus) is established in the DC area. Invasive plants like kudzu and Japanese knotweed create ideal mosquito habitat [citation:7][citation:2].

DC Law – Landlord Screen Requirements (March 15 – November 15)

Washington DC has one of the most comprehensive landlord-tenant screen laws in the country [citation:14]. DC law requires landlords to:

  • Put screens on all doors and windows from March 15 to November 15 of every year [citation:14]
  • Ensure screens don't have holes or cracks [citation:14]
  • Have at least 16 meshes to the inch (16 mesh per inch) — or a screen that is just as effective [citation:14]
  • Provide hinged screen doors that open outwards and are self-closing [citation:14]
  • Alternatives to screens may only be used with DOB approval [citation:14]

Under DC housing enforcement and general habitability principles, rental units must be safe and free from conditions that harm health, including openings that allow insect infestation [citation:11]. Landlords must replace torn or missing window screens that cause pest problems or allow entry of pests [citation:11].

If a landlord fails to provide or repair screens, tenants can file a complaint with the DC Department of Buildings (DOB) or contact the Office of the Tenant Advocate (OTA) for help understanding rights, mediation options, and referrals to legal assistance [citation:11].

Under federal fair housing rules, tenants with a disability can request a reasonable accommodation (for example, installing finer screening to avoid insect-borne illness) in writing. The Fair Housing Act may require the landlord to grant reasonable accommodations even if the lease or building rules would otherwise prohibit the change [citation:11].
